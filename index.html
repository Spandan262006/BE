4.0

Overview — Why Experiments?

Behavioural economics sits at the intersection of psychology and economics. While theory tells us how rational "Econs" should behave, experiments reveal how real humans actually behave. The experimental approach is the discipline's primary tool for generating reliable knowledge about decision-making.

🧠 Core Purpose

Experiments allow researchers to observe real human choices in controlled settings, test theoretical predictions, and discover behavioural facts (like self-control issues, cognitive biases, and social preferences). The key advantage is causal inference — by manipulating one variable while holding everything else constant, you can prove that A causes B, not just that A and B are correlated.

📝 Exam Insight

The critical distinction in this module is correlation vs. causation. A survey can show a correlation ("gym members are healthier"). An experiment can prove causation ("going to the gym makes people healthier"). Experiments are superior because they eliminate confounding variables.

Behavioural Facts discovered through experiments include:

  • People exhibit self-control problems — they plan to exercise but don't follow through.
  • Cognitive biases (anchoring, loss aversion) reliably influence choices.
  • Social preferences (fairness, reciprocity) shape economic decisions.
  • Human strategic thinking is limited, not infinitely rational.
⚠️ Key Distinction

Unlike observational data (where you watch what happens naturally), experiments let you actively manipulate one thing and measure its effect. This is why the experimental approach is called the "gold standard" for causal inference in both medicine and economics.

4.1.1

History of Randomized Experiments

The idea of testing hypotheses through controlled, randomized experiments originated in medicine before migrating to economics and social sciences. Here is the full historical arc you need to know:

1774
James Lind — The First Randomized Trial
Conducted one of the earliest controlled experiments to find a cure for scurvy among sailors. He divided sailors into groups and administered different treatments (including lemons to one group), comparing outcomes. This established the basic logic of randomization — give one group the treatment, give another group nothing (control), and see what happens.
Early 1900s
Ronald Fisher — Formalizing Randomization
The statistician who gave randomized experiments their mathematical foundations. His books Statistical Methods for Research Workers and The Design of Experiments established that random assignment (randomly deciding who gets the treatment) is the key mechanism for causal inference. Fisher's work is why we trust RCTs today.
1948
Austin Bradford Hill — Medical RCTs Enter Modern Era
Used Randomized Control Trials (RCTs) to test streptomycin as a treatment for tuberculosis (TB). This is considered one of the first properly conducted medical RCTs in the modern sense — formally randomizing who gets the drug and who doesn't.
1954
Jonas Salk — Polio Vaccine & Double-Blind RCT
Developed the polio vaccine through a massive double-blind RCT — neither the participants nor the researchers administering the vaccine knew who received the real vaccine vs. the placebo. Used Fisher's exact test to measure treatment effects. This remains one of the largest and most famous clinical trials in history.
📝 Must Know for Exams

Lind (1774) → scurvy/lemons. Fisher → formalized randomization. Hill (1948) → streptomycin/TB. Salk (1954) → polio, double-blind, Fisher's exact test. These are frequently tested as short-answer or MCQ questions.

4.1.2

Social & Laboratory Experiments

These experiments moved the methodology into economics and social science, studying individual decisions and strategic interactions rather than just medical outcomes.

🏛️ Individual Decision-Making Experiments

1931 — Thurstone
Mapping Indifference Curves in a Lab
Used laboratory experiments to empirically deduce indifference curves between pairs of goods (e.g., hats and shoes). This was one of the first attempts to test fundamental microeconomic theory about consumer preferences in a controlled setting.
1944 — Von Neumann & Morgenstern
Utility Curves and Risk Behaviour
Proposed using experiments to construct utility curves and empirically test theoretical predictions about how people make decisions under risk. Their work laid the foundation for Expected Utility Theory — and later for experiments that showed humans violate these predictions (e.g., via Prospect Theory).

🤝 Social Interaction Experiments

1950 — Dresher & Flood
The Prisoner's Dilemma in the Lab
First implemented the famous Prisoner's Dilemma in a laboratory setting. Key finding: human participants rarely reach the pure Nash Equilibrium (mutual defection), but they also don't perfectly achieve the socially efficient outcome (mutual cooperation). Real humans are more cooperative than theory predicts, but imperfectly so.
1982 — Güth, Schmittberger & Schwarze
The Ultimatum Game
Developed the Ultimatum Game, a cornerstone of behavioural bargaining research. In this game, Player 1 proposes how to split a sum of money; Player 2 either accepts (both get the split) or rejects (both get nothing). Theory predicts Player 1 should offer the minimum and Player 2 should accept anything. Reality: players offer ~40-50%, and low offers are rejected out of fairness concerns.

🏥 Policy-Oriented Social Experiments

1971–1982 — RAND Corporation
Health Insurance Experiment
One of the largest social experiments ever conducted. Studied the causal effect of health insurance coverage on usage. Key finding: increasing cost-sharing (copay) — making patients pay a portion of costs — led to fewer hospital and dental visits and lower overall spending. Crucially, this did not necessarily harm health outcomes, suggesting some healthcare is discretionary.
1977 — Burtless & Hausman
The Negative Income Tax Experiment (US/Canada)
Tested the effect of providing unconditional cash transfers to the poor. Two surprising findings: (1) providing basic income had no significant effect on individual labor supply — people didn't stop working; (2) it unexpectedly increased divorce rates, as financial independence gave partners the ability to leave unhappy marriages.
📝 Exam Tip — Common Question Format

You may be asked: "What did the RAND experiment find?" → Higher copay → less usage, without harming health. Or: "What did the Negative Income Tax experiment show?" → No effect on labor supply, but increased divorce rates. Know both the experiment AND its unexpected findings.

4.2.1

Typology of Experiments

Not all experiments are identical. They differ in where they are conducted (the environment) and who participates (the subject pool). Here are the four main types:

🧪 1. Laboratory Experiments

Conducted in a controlled lab setting, usually with students or volunteers on a university campus.

Precision
High degree of control over data collection. Every aspect of the environment is controlled — the room, the instructions, the timing, the payoffs.
Incentivization
Participants are paid based on their decisions (e.g., whatever they win in the game). This ensures their choices reflect real preferences, not hypothetical ones.
WEIRD Criticism
Subjects are typically Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic. Results may not generalize to the rest of humanity.
⚠️ WEIRD — Know This Acronym Cold

Western · Educated · Industrialized · Rich · Democratic. This is the standard criticism of lab experiments — they over-rely on a very specific, unrepresentative slice of humanity (usually Western university students). This raises concerns about external validity — can we generalize findings to, say, subsistence farmers in rural India?

🌾 2. Lab-in-the-Field (Artefactual Field Experiments)

Researchers take the controlled lab setup out into the real world — for example, setting up a mini-lab in a village community centre and running experiments with local farmers or residents as subjects.

🧠 Key Advantage

This bridges the gap between theory and real-world context. You get the precision of a lab (controlled instructions, monetary incentives) with a more representative subject pool (not WEIRD students). It resolves the external validity problem partially.

💻 3. Online and Survey Experiments

Conducted via platforms like Amazon MTurk, Prolific, or Qualtrics — participants complete tasks online from anywhere in the world.

✅ Advantages ❌ Disadvantages
Low cost, fast, large scale No control over environment
High geographic diversity (generalizable) Risk of AI/bot responses
Can reach specific demographics easily Time-limited (20–30 min max attention span)

🏙️ 4. Field Experiments and RCTs

Participants are observed in their everyday, natural environments — schools, factories, hospitals, markets — often without knowing they are being studied.

🧠 Two Critical Biases They Eliminate

Social Desirability Bias: When people know they're being watched, they change their behaviour to look good. Field experiments avoid this because participants don't know they're in a study.

Hawthorne Effect: The well-known phenomenon where people change their behaviour simply because they are being observed, regardless of the actual treatment. Field experiments in natural settings minimize this.

⚠️ Field Experiment vs. RCT — Subtle But Important

Field experiments often test precise theoretical predictions (e.g., does a certain incentive change effort?). RCTs (Randomized Control Trials) are more directly linked to evaluating specific policies in the real world (e.g., do free bed nets reduce malaria?). Both are conducted in the field — the distinction lies in their purpose. RCTs are considered the "gold standard" for policy evaluation — associated with Nobel laureates Banerjee, Duflo, and Kremer.

4.2.2

Non-Randomized Experiments

Sometimes, pure randomization is impossible — you cannot randomly assign someone to be born into poverty, or randomly assign a country to have a civil war. In these cases, researchers use clever alternative designs:

🌍 Natural Experiments

🧠 What Is It?

A natural experiment exploits an external shock or naturally-occurring event that randomly (or as-if randomly) affects some people but not others. The researcher didn't create the experiment — nature or policy did.

🔍 Classic Example: Rwanda Terrain Study

Researchers used Rwanda's terrain (mountainous vs. flat areas) to study the impact of radio nation-building programs on inter-ethnic trust. Terrain is essentially random — it determines who could and couldn't receive radio signals — so radio access was "as good as random." This allowed causal inference about radio's effect on trust without any experimental manipulation.

📅 Quasi-Experiments

🧠 What Is It?

Compare the same group before and after an event (a policy change, a harvest, a life event). The group serves as its own control — eliminating individual-level variation.

🔍 Classic Example: Farmers & Cognitive Bandwidth

Researchers studied farmers' cognitive ability before vs. after harvest. Before harvest, farmers are poor and financially stressed (scarce resources → tunnel vision). After harvest, they have money. The same farmer performed better on cognitive tests after harvest — showing that poverty itself impairs cognition, not innate ability. The harvest timing is a natural event that creates the quasi-experimental comparison.

🌐 Cross-Cultural Studies

🧠 What Is It?

Run the same experiment in different societies with different cultural norms, structures, or histories. If behaviour differs systematically across societies, it suggests behaviour is shaped by nurture (culture/environment) rather than nature (biology/universal psychology).

🔍 Classic Example: Gneezy & List — Matrilineal vs. Patriarchal

Compared competition preferences between two societies: a matrilineal tribe (Khasi in India, where women inherit property and hold social power) and a patriarchal tribe. In the matrilineal society, women were equally competitive as men. In the patriarchal society, men dominated competitive environments. Conclusion: the gender gap in competitiveness is not biologically hardwired — it is a product of social norms. Behaviour is nurture, not nature.

📝 Exam Tip

The three types exist because randomization is sometimes impossible. Know the defining feature of each: Natural = external shock exploited; Quasi = before-after comparison; Cross-cultural = same experiment, different societies to test nature vs. nurture.

4.3.1

Historical Ethical Failures

Modern research ethics emerged directly in response to horrific abuses. Understanding these cases is essential — they explain why every rule that follows exists.

1943–1945
🇩🇪 Nazi Experiments
Medical torture of twins and other prisoners in concentration camps under the pretext of genetic research. Conducted without consent, causing immense suffering and death. Led directly to the Nuremberg Code, the first international standard for human experimentation.
1932–1972
🩺 Tuskegee Syphilis Study
African American men with syphilis were deliberately denied penicillin (a known cure) so researchers could study the untreated progression of the disease. Continued for 40 years. Left survivors with serious health consequences and devastated trust in medical institutions.
1961
⚡ Milgram Obedience Experiment
Participants were pressured by an authority figure to administer what they believed were lethal electric shocks to another person. ~65% complied to the maximum voltage. Revealed frightening obedience to authority — but caused severe psychological distress to participants without proper safeguards.
1971
🏛️ Stanford Prison Experiment
Students were randomly assigned as "guards" or "prisoners" in a mock prison. The guard group became sadistic and abusive within days, causing severe psychological stress to the "prisoners." The experiment had to be terminated early. No adequate safeguards or oversight existed.
⚠️ Why This Matters for Exams

These cases are the motivation for the Belmont Report (1979) and all modern IRB requirements. If asked "why was the Belmont Report necessary?" or "why do IRBs exist?" — these four cases are your answer. Know each experiment's year and its specific ethical violation.

4.3.2

The Belmont Report (1979)

Published in 1979 in direct response to the ethical abuses above, the Belmont Report established the three foundational pillars of modern research ethics. Every research protocol involving human subjects must satisfy all three.

👤
1. Respect for Persons
Participants are autonomous agents who must be fully informed and freely consenting. Requires Informed Consent. Special protections for those with diminished autonomy (minors, cognitively impaired individuals).
⚕️
2. Beneficence
The "Do No Harm" principle. Researchers must systematically maximize potential benefits and minimize all possible harms and risks to participants. The benefits to society must outweigh the risks to individuals.
⚖️
3. Justice
Fair distribution of the burdens and benefits of research. Vulnerable populations (the poor, prisoners, minorities) must not be exploited to generate knowledge that primarily benefits others. Research burdens should not fall disproportionately on disadvantaged groups.
🧠 Deep Dive: Informed Consent

Informed Consent is a critical application of "Respect for Persons." It is an ongoing process, not a one-time signature. Participants must be told: (1) the purpose and procedures of the study, (2) any foreseeable risks, (3) potential benefits, (4) that participation is voluntary and they can withdraw at any time. If new information emerges mid-study, consent must be re-obtained.

🔍 Connecting History to Principles

Nazi Experiments violated all three pillars (especially Respect for Persons — zero consent). Tuskegee violated Beneficence (harm was done, cure withheld) and Justice (exploited a marginalized group). Milgram violated Respect for Persons and Beneficence (psychological harm, deception without proper debriefing). Stanford violated Beneficence (inadequate risk minimization).

📝 Exam Tip — The Three Pillars

The exam will almost certainly test the three Belmont pillars. Memorize: Respect for Persons (autonomy + informed consent) · Beneficence (do no harm, maximize benefit) · Justice (fair distribution, don't exploit vulnerable groups). Know a one-line description of each.

4.3.3

Modern Requirements & IRBs

The Belmont principles are enforced in practice through Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) and a set of formal requirements that all researchers must follow.

🏛️ What is an IRB?

🧠 Definition

An Institutional Review Board (IRB) is an independent committee — with a minimum of 5 members — that reviews, approves, and monitors research involving human subjects. No study can begin without IRB clearance. The IRB's job is to ensure the research satisfies all three Belmont pillars before a single participant is recruited.

📋 Review Categories

✅ Exempt

Minimal risk to participants. Examples: standard educational surveys, anonymous questionnaires, observation of public behaviour. No ongoing oversight required.

🔍 Expedited

Risk is no more than minimal. Reviewed by just a subset of the full board rather than requiring a full committee meeting. Faster approval process.

🔴 Full Board Review

Required when risks to participants exceed minimal levels. Entire committee reviews and votes on approval. Highest scrutiny level.

🚫 Deception — A Special Rule in Economics

⚠️ Critical Rule — Economics Prohibits Deception

In economics, there is a dominant norm against the use of objectively false information in experiments. You cannot lie to participants (e.g., "you are actually playing against a human" when they're playing against a computer). This protects the discipline's credibility — if participants expect to be deceived, their behaviour in all future economics experiments becomes unreliable.

In other disciplines (like psychology), deception may be used with permission — but it requires a mandatory debriefing session afterward, where participants are told the truth about the experiment's real purpose.

💾 Data Management Requirements

🧠 What You Need to Plan For

Every research protocol must include a clear data management plan covering: (1) Anonymization — removing identifiable information; (2) Password protection — securing data against unauthorized access; (3) Data retention policy — research data must typically be retained for a minimum of 3 years after the study ends, then properly destroyed.

📝 Quick-Fire Facts for Exams

IRB minimum size: 5 members. Economics rule: No deception. If other fields use deception: mandatory debriefing. Data retention: minimum 3 years. Three review types: Exempt → Expedited → Full Board (in order of increasing risk).

4.4

Project Design — Putting It All Together

This section covers how to design and analyze a valid behavioural experiment — the skills tested in your project/assignment component.

⚙️ Core Design Requirements

1
Causality Over Correlation
A simple survey is not sufficient. You must have a randomized component — randomly assigning participants to conditions — to establish causation. Without randomization, you can only show correlation (which could be driven by a third variable).
2
Experimental Design: Treatment vs. Control
Must include at least one Treatment group (receives the intervention/manipulation) and one Control group (receives nothing or a neutral condition). The difference in outcomes between these groups is your treatment effect — the causal impact of your intervention.
3
Balance Table — Did Randomization Work?
Before analysing outcomes, you must verify that your groups are actually comparable. A Balance Table compares baseline covariates (background characteristics: age, gender, income, education) between treatment and control groups. If randomization worked correctly, these characteristics should be statistically similar across groups. Any significant difference suggests the randomization failed.
4
Summary Table — Did the Intervention Work?
Compares outcome variables between treatment and control. Uses appropriate statistical tests: t-test for continuous variables (e.g., income, test scores); Chi-Square test for binary/categorical variables (e.g., did they donate: yes/no). The difference in outcomes is your estimate of the causal treatment effect.
5
Hypothesis Testing — The Null Hypothesis
Hypotheses are formally written in the negative form: the Null Hypothesis (H₀) states "the treatment has no effect." You then try to reject the null hypothesis. Standard threshold: alpha (α) = 0.05 — if your p-value is less than 0.05, you reject H₀ and conclude the treatment had a statistically significant effect at the 95% confidence level.
🧠 How It All Connects

Think of the sequence: Randomize (assign to groups) → Balance Table (confirm groups are identical before treatment) → Implement treatment → Summary Table (compare outcomes) → Hypothesis Test (determine statistical significance). This is the full chain of a valid experiment from design to conclusion.

🔑 Key Statistical Terms You Must Know

Null Hypothesis (H₀)
The default assumption that the treatment has no effect. Written negatively. You attempt to reject it with evidence.
Alpha (α) = 0.05
The threshold for statistical significance. p < 0.05 means <5% probability the result occurred by chance → reject H₀.
Balance Table
Tests that treatment & control groups have similar baseline characteristics. Proves randomization worked.
Summary Table
Compares outcomes between groups. t-test for continuous; Chi-Square for binary.
External Validity
Can the findings be generalized beyond the specific sample/setting? WEIRD lab experiments have low external validity.
Internal Validity
Does the experiment actually measure what it claims to? High in randomized lab experiments. Can be threatened by confounders.
📝 Exam Tip — Balance vs. Summary Tables

Students often confuse these two. Remember: Balance Table = BEFORE treatment, checking COVARIATES (background). Summary Table = AFTER treatment, checking OUTCOMES. The balance table proves your randomization was valid; the summary table shows whether your intervention worked.

📚 Quick Revision Summary

Key Definitions

  • WEIRD: Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic — the unrepresentative subject pool of most lab experiments
  • External Validity: The degree to which results can be generalized beyond the sample & setting
  • Incentivization: Paying participants based on their actual choices to ensure real-world relevance
  • Double-Blind: Neither participant nor administrator knows who is in treatment vs. control group
  • Social Desirability Bias: Participants behave "nicely" when they know they're being watched
  • Hawthorne Effect: Behaviour changes simply because of being observed, regardless of treatment
  • Null Hypothesis: Default statement that treatment has no effect; written negatively to be rejected

  • Dresher & Flood (1950): First Prisoner's Dilemma in lab; humans rarely hit Nash Eq.
